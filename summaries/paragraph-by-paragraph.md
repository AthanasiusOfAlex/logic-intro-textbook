# Introduction {-}

This text is for students taking a course on logic. Most people intuitively understand that logic relates to correct thinking, but this course aims to develop precision and thoroughness in forming concepts. The central question is "What precisely is logic?" This introduction will provide a precise and rigorous answer by introducing ideas from epistemology, metaphysics, and philosophical anthropology. These topics are not part of logic's material object but are necessary for understanding what logic actually is. Logic is taught at the beginning of philosophical studies to lay groundwork for the study of metaphysics.

This introduction is divided into three chapters. The first chapter introduces the theory behind sciences and arts to show that logic can be classified as both. The second chapter introduces basic concepts of philosophical anthropology to better understand what an habitus (such as a science like logic) is. The final chapter provides a detailed look into the precise definition of logic.

# Logic as a Science and an Art

## Sciences vs. Disciplines

Most people think of logic as a discipline, meaning a body of knowledge that can be acquired through study. This view is abstract and ignores the actual existence of logic in reality. This abstract approach is how most people view all academic fields because they encounter them through formal education.

A field of study is not merely an abstract discipline but produces a real change in the intellect of the person who learns it. The Schrödinger equation from quantum mechanics illustrates this point: understanding it requires a stable change in the intellect, not just raw human capability. This modification or ordering of the intellect is called a habitus. An intellectual habitus that helps us know things rather than do things is called a science.

The science of quantum mechanics refers to the permanent modification of the intellect acquired by studying it, not primarily the discipline itself. This usage follows classical philosophers who used episteme (Greek) and scientia (Latin) to mean an intellectual habitus that permits knowledge acquisition. A scientia is a real, concrete attribute of a person, not merely an abstract body of knowledge.

Logic is also a science (episteme): an actual intellectual habitus that exists in the logician's person, not merely abstract rules. It is useful to explain what a science (episteme) is and how it differs from other habitus.

## The Essential Characteristics of any Science

Medieval thinkers, following Aristotle's Posterior Analytics, described science (episteme) as cognitio certa per causas (certain knowledge through causes). Science differs from mere opinion (doxa) because one can link known facts to their causes.

Medical science provides an example: humans knew for millennia that diseases like the Black Death were contagious and used quarantine. However, this was correct opinion, not science. Medical science became true science only when the germ theory revealed the actual causes of disease in the late nineteenth century. A person today might know that handwashing prevents infection without knowing that bacteria cause the infection, meaning they have correct opinion (doxa) but not science (episteme).

Chemistry provides another example: scientists observed that chemical species combined in certain proportions but did not understand why. For instance, combining hydrogen and chlorine in certain ratios produced unexpected results. Only when atomic theory and molecular structure were discovered did the phenomena become satisfactorily explained through their causes.

Although seeking causes distinguishes science from opinion, Aristotle identifies three other essential characteristics: science must be certain (leading to true knowledge without error), it must study what can be attributed to its object in a necessary way, and it must be universally applicable to its object.

Chemistry illustrates these characteristics: mixing sodium bicarbonate with hydrochloric acid certainly produces salt and carbon dioxide. This result is necessary (it cannot be otherwise). The result is universal because it applies to all similar situations, and chemistry studies only what can be attributed per se (essentially) to the entities studied. Chemistry is a science because it seeks to identify the causes of these properties in atomic and molecular structures.

The degree of certainty, necessity, and universal applicability varies among sciences. Pure mathematics attains greater certainty than social sciences like psychology or economics. Even when complete certainty is unattainable, a science provides useful knowledge to the degree it is certain, necessary, and universal. Economics, for example, accurately predicts general trends even if it cannot predict individual consumer behavior. Science cannot account for what is per accidens (completely incidental).

A science or episteme is an intellectual habitus with four essential characteristics. First and most fundamentally, it seeks to connect phenomena to their underlying causes, which distinguishes science from opinion. Second, it provides certain knowledge without error. Third, it accounts for phenomena that are necessarily (per se) linked to the object of study, not merely incidentally (per accidens). Fourth, it applies universally to its object of study, not to singulars and particulars.

Two things follow: to determine whether an intellectual habitus is a science, showing these four characteristics is sufficient. False information results from erroneous opinion (doxa), not from science or episteme in the strict sense.

## Speculative vs. Practical Sciences

Science as defined by ancient and Medieval philosophers (as scientia or episteme) includes many more fields than customarily called "science" today. By this definition, science includes mathematics, metaphysics, theology, and ethics, not just empirical fields like biology, chemistry, or physics. Each field must have a specific object of study (genus subiectum) and appropriate methods.

Sciences can be categorized according to their finality. Modern parlance distinguishes pure sciences (biology, chemistry, physics) from applied sciences (medical science, engineering). This division applies to all sciences in the classical sense: speculative sciences seek knowledge for its own sake, while practical sciences seek applications of knowledge to action.

Table 1 shows examples of speculative and practical sciences. Speculative sciences (left column) seek knowledge for its own sake: a biologist can study robin nesting behavior without any practical purpose. The character of the work as biology does not change based on ulterior motives or lack thereof. The same applies to chemistry, physics, mathematics, and metaphysics (the most speculative science with no direct practical application).

Practical sciences (right column) have a specific kind of operation or action in mind. They produce knowledge, but knowledge is a means to action, not an end in itself. Studying physics alone will not teach you how to build a bridge; that requires mechanical engineering. Studying biology alone is insufficient to cure disease; that requires medical science. Many practical sciences can be paired with speculative sciences from which they derive. Ethics is a practical science because its purpose is to direct our actions: discovering which behaviors are compatible with human happiness.

## Is Logic a Science?

Logic is clearly an academic discipline, but the question arises whether it is a science like biology, chemistry, and mathematics. To answer this, we must apply the definition: a science is cognitio certa per causas.

Logic is a cognitio (habitus) of the intellect: without logic, the intellect would be impeded from acquiring certain knowledge. In the complete absence of logic, we would be unable to acquire any knowledge at all. Systematic study of logic makes our thinking easier, more ordered, and less prone to error.

Logic provides sure and certain knowledge by linking what it studies to its causes. Logic seeks the causes of knowledge: through logic, I can be sure that conclusion C follows from premises A and B. This logical causality differs from the ontological causality (causes of being) sought in other sciences, but it is still causality.

Logic provides certain knowledge without error, supplies knowledge that follows necessarily from its object, and applies universally to all works of our intellect. The very purpose of logic is to facilitate reasoning and make it less prone to error. Logic provides tools to see that conclusions are correct and could not be otherwise. Logic applies to all works of our intellect without exception.

There are two distinct entities essential for knowledge: our intellect (the faculty permitting acts of knowledge) and the intellectual habitus (logic). Intellect, called our reason (ratio in Latin, logos in Greek), is a kind of "natural logic." Logic in the strict sense refers to an acquired habitus that helps the intellect operate. This scientific logic can be acquired imperfectly (intuitive grasp from mere use of reason) or perfectly/systematically (which this course attempts to do).

We must conclude that logic is indeed a science.

## Logica utens vs. logica docens

Another question arises: is logic a speculative science or a practical science. Speculative science seeks knowledge for its own sake, and logic seems to fit this description. St. Thomas Aquinas describes logic's purpose as directing the act of reason so that man may proceed in an orderly manner, more easily, and without error. This art is logic, the rational science.

The purpose of logic is to order the individual actions of our intellect to make knowledge acquisition easier and less error-prone. When someone acquires the science of logic, distinguishing truth from falsehood becomes easier. Logic is our instrument for discovering true knowledge and ensuring it is true. We can categorize logic as a speculative science, though unique: its ultimate goal is not knowledge of its object (the works of reason) but knowledge of objects in all other sciences. We study logic to use it in biology, mathematics, physics, engineering, and every other science, not to study the works of reason for their own sake. Logic is instrumentally speculative: it seeks knowledge for its own sake strictly as a means or instrument of obtaining that knowledge.

A new question arises: if logic is a means to obtaining knowledge, and obtaining knowledge is an action, doesn't that make logic a practical science. It does. This means "speculative" and "practical" are not mutually exclusive: a science can be both.

This is possible because a science, as an intellectual habitus, makes the intellect capable of actions it could not do before. An habitus is required when special difficulty exists in acquiring knowledge: for instance, one only needs to learn quantum physics to understand things like the Schrödinger equation. Many speculative sciences can be paired with practical sciences (mechanics with mechanical engineering, human biology with medical science). A distinct applied science is necessary because application is not obvious: mechanics tells how to calculate forces but not how to build a bridge. When application of speculative knowledge poses particular difficulty, a distinct habitus (science) is required.

With logic, the situation differs. Since logic studies the very works of our reason and the proper construction of relations between them, merely studying logic's object immediately hones our ability to reason.

Logic is both a speculative science and a practical science: logic is speculative insofar as its end goal is knowledge, but its mode of achieving that goal is practical (it teaches us how to know). For this reason, logic is a modally practical science.

## Is Logic Also an Art (techne)?

Aquinas calls logic an "art" (ars in Latin, techne in Greek), which seems surprising after demonstrating that logic is a science. It may also surprise readers that Aquinas groups it with painting, music, sculpture, and theater.

For Aquinas, an art is any intellectual habitus that results immediately in action: recta ratio factibilium (right reason applied to things that are made). It is use of reason or intellect (ratio) that is properly ordered (recta or "right") to accomplishing determined operations or actions (factibilium).

Examples include sculpture: to carve marble into a statue of David requires acquiring an habitus (the art of sculpture) before setting chisel to stone. The same reasoning applies to violinists, opera singers, painters, and photographers. For Aquinas, art includes any habitus necessary for skilled action, so surgeons, architects, athletes, lawyers, carpenters, writers, orators, accountants, and entrepreneurs are all "artists" by this definition.

Arts are distinguished by the kind of action they produce. Mechanical arts produce something external (painting produces frescoes or canvasses, carpentry produces tables and chairs). Liberal arts produce something internal to the intellect (practicing law produces legal precedents, decisions, motions, and verdicts, not physical works).

Arts have characteristics in common, illustrated by carpentry: a carpenter takes raw material (wood) and fashions it into finished products (tables and cabinets) by applying learned techniques.

This generalizes to every art: the practitioner takes an indeterminate entity (raw material) that can be fashioned as desired but need not be, and transforms it using determinate and universal means to produce the desired product. An artist's task is to take indeterminate "matter" and remove its indetermination. When Michelangelo received marble blocks, the possible statues he could make were infinite (fully indeterminate), but as he worked, they became fully determinate (a Pieta).

Any habitus with these two characteristics can be classified as an art or techne. With mechanical arts this is obvious, but the same holds for liberal arts: a defense lawyer formulates a defense from case facts and legal knowledge, an orator formulates a speech from language possibilities, a composer forms a musical composition from tones, rhythms, dynamics, and instrumentation.

An habitus is an art if it fulfills two characteristics: it takes indeterminate matter that does not infallibly attain its fully determined form, and it transforms that matter into a finished product using determinate and universal means. Logic qualifies: (1) logic takes works of reason (concepts, enunciations, arguments) and constructs relations between them according to strict rules; since reason can fall into error, these works constitute the indeterminate matter of logic. (2) Among all possible relations, logic constructs the correct relations using universal and determined rules to produce true knowledge. Logic is indeed an art: a liberal art, as it does not produce an external product.

## How Logic Manages to Be All Three at Once

Ordinarily, speculative sciences, practical sciences, and arts can be clearly distinguished even when their subject matter is related. Human biology is the speculative science at the root of medical matters, medical science is the practical science applying biology to disease treatment, and medicine is the art applying medical science to concrete treatment acts. Three distinct habitus are at play because neither application is trivial. A surgeon cannot be sufficiently trained by acquiring only the speculative science of human biology. Even detailed knowledge of medical science is insufficient: he must spend years learning how surgeries are done yet still be unprepared for actual surgery. He becomes a surgeon only by acquiring the art of surgery, which requires speculative and practical knowledge plus hand coordination training. What renders the three habitus distinct is the considerable gap between speculative science and practical science, and between practical science and art.

With logic, we use our intellect to study works produced by that same intellect, so the distance present in other fields is absent. The speculative aspect of logic as a science cannot be really distinguished from its practical aspect. The art of logic cannot be really distinguished from the science of logic. Formal scientific instruction in logic (logica docens) studies an object intrinsic to the very intellect studying it, resulting immediately in improved exercise of logic (logica utens). Later in the course, you will learn to distinguish and relate concepts, relate enunciations, and form and analyze syllogisms. You can use these skills immediately without needing an additional science or distinct art to apply them.

## How Logic is Necessary

Logic is necessary in a particular way. As Aquinas says, "the necessary is that which cannot not be."

Aquinas argues there are four kinds of necessity. First, something can be necessary by an intrinsic principle (material or formal): material necessity occurs when we say everything composed from contraries must be corrupted; formal necessity occurs when we say the three angles of a triangle must equal two right angles (this is natural or absolute necessity). Second, something is unable not to be by an extrinsic principle (end or agent): an end is necessary when something cannot be obtained without it or cannot be obtained well without it (food is necessary for life, a horse for a journey; this is necessity of end or utility). Necessity regarding agents occurs when someone is forced by an agent so they cannot do the contrary (necessity of coercion).

Material necessity concerns limitations that matter places on material beings: they take up space, must exist in a single location, and are corruptible. Formal necessity (natural or absolute necessity) stems from substantial or accidental forms of an entity and can be of two kinds: it can stem from the entity's essence and be part of its definition (man is necessarily rational), or it can be a property stemming from that essence (man is capable of laughing). Final necessity refers to means required to obtain an end (what we generally call "needing" something). Aquinas observes this need can be strict or moral. Food is a strict necessity for life. Taking a car to go somewhere 10 miles away is not a strict necessity but is a moral necessity (much better unless you enjoy walking).

Logic is essential for obtaining knowledge. Natural logic (another name for reason itself) is a strict necessity for knowing anything. For obtaining systematic scientific knowledge, such knowledge can be obtained imperfectly (understanding only easiest conclusions with fuzzy dominance) or perfectly (satisfying all conditions of possessing a science with necessary rigor). For imperfect grasp of a science, natural logic is sufficient (though scientific logic is better). Scientific logic is a moral necessity of end for this knowledge. However, scientific logic is a strict necessity of end for "perfect" scientific knowledge.

We conclude that logic is simultaneously an instrumentally speculative science, a modally practical science, and an art whose object is the works of our reason. Natural logic is a strict necessity for all knowledge whatsoever. Logic studied scientifically is a great help for obtaining scientific knowledge and absolutely essential for "perfect" scientific knowledge. What remains is to discuss in greater detail the object of logic, which will be done after a brief discussion of key concepts in philosophical anthropology.
# A Primer on the Metaphysics of Man

This chapter explores essentials of philosophical anthropology, which is the metaphysical study of mankind. The course studies logic but also introduces terminology and ideas that form the basis of philosophical inquiry. Laying philosophical bases makes it easier to understand the definition of logic.

Philosophical anthropology is distinguished from cultural anthropology, which is empirical rather than philosophical in nature.

## What are metaphysics and anthropology?

This section provides a short introduction to metaphysics and philosophical anthropology for readers encountering these sciences for the first time.

Metaphysics is the study of entities not according to particular aspects but simply because they are. Metaphysics studies existing things as being. Metaphysics is the most general of sciences. No created entity is excluded from metaphysics. Metaphysics seeks properties that can be attributed to all entities without exception. Metaphysics seeks the causes on which all things depend. Metaphysics studies created entities, not God. God is of interest as the First and Ultimate extrinsic Cause of all entities.

Aristotle's definition states that metaphysics studies Being as Being and the properties inherent in it. In languages like English that do not distinguish well between being as something that exists and being as a synonym of to be, this definition is often mistranslated. Metaphysics studies existing things insofar as they are, not primarily the perfection or attribute of existing things that we call being.

Logic will touch on the transcendentals when covering the logic of the concept.

God is not a mere entity in the strict sense but is Being Itself.

The pattern that the cause is not the object of a science is common throughout all sciences. Biology studies organisms and their properties, not the chemical causes of those properties, but relies on chemistry to understand those properties. Metaphysics studies created entities, not God, but knowing about God helps the science understand entities.

Aristotle called this science various names including first Science, theology, and wisdom, but not metaphysics. The name metaphysics was given by compilers of Aristotle's works because they placed those treatises after Aristotle's works on physics. Despite the trivial origin of the name, it is appropriate because metaphysics is the only philosophical discipline that can transcend beyond the physical world.

When metaphysical principles are applied to mankind, the resulting science is philosophical anthropology. Philosophical anthropology studies man insofar as he is, or insofar as he is man. Philosophical anthropology seeks properties that apply universally to man and links them to their ultimate intrinsic and extrinsic causes. For our purposes, it is enough to look at some of the intrinsic causes of man.

## Entities (*Entia*), Substances, and Accidents.

The term entity translates the Latin ens, which classical and Medieval philosophers used to translate Aristotle's present participle of the Greek verb to be. An entity is simply something that is, or exists. Aquinas describes an entity as that which is or that which has being. The term being refers to a perfection of the entity, not a mere logical fact.

In metaphysics, there is a difference in meaning between existence and being. Existence is the mere fact that something is, whereas being refers to the ontological perfection that renders existence possible. Aristotle and St. Thomas Aquinas maintain this distinct meaning. Being refers to a perfection, so the verb to be is preferred when referring to entities.

Outside of God, who is Being Itself, anything that is at all is an entity. There are nothing but entities, and anything else does not exist. An oak tree is clearly an entity. The oak tree has attributes and properties such as height, width, weight, place, textures, colors, time, and configuration. These attributes can be predicated of the oak tree using the verb to be, indicating that these attributes enjoy a certain type of being. The attributes do not enjoy the same kind or degree of being as the tree itself. The oak tree is independent and subsists. The attributes do not exist on their own but are always in another entity, meaning they inhere in another entity.

This reasoning discovers the first division of entities. Some entities called substances are subsistent and exist by themselves. Other entities called accidents are mere attributes or modifications of a substance and are merely inherent in a substance. Substance and accident are best thought of as constituent principles of a thing. The oak tree has an intrinsic principle that makes it an oak tree, called the substance or essence. The tree has distinct principles that give it various perfections such as height, weight, and location.

Aristotle's term accident is derived from the Greek preposition meaning with and the verb meaning to step or walk. The verb in ancient Greek meant to stand with or beside. The form used is the perfect participle.

The term substance can take on slightly different but closely related meanings depending on context. Substances as fully constituted wholes were initially distinguished from accidents as different kinds of entities. Medieval philosophers used the term supposit to denote this meaning. Substance can also be thought of as an intrinsic constitutive principle that is composed with the accidents. Western European philosophers generally used the term essence to denote this usage. The two meanings are so closely related that Aristotle uses the very same word for both.

Substance, whether as supposit or as essence, is always the answer to the question what is it. Only substances subsist, so only they constitute a full answer to this question. Unlike accidents, only substance constitutes what a thing is simply because it is, not because of some perfection or attribute.

All created realities are rightly called entities because they are. Substances are the only entities to subsist or be by themselves, and thus are entities par excellence. Substances are constituted by an intrinsic principle called the essence that gives each substance its definition. The essence is composed with various perfections or attributes called accidents. Entities are always real; anything that lacks being entirely is not properly called an entity.

## The Intrinsic Composition of Man: Faculties, *Habitus*, and Operations

That is enough introduction to intrinsic metaphysical principles to begin applying them to man, who is the only creature that makes use of logic. A human being is a substance that subsists. Therefore, like any substance, he is composed of an essence and various accidents. His essence is composed of prime matter and substantial form, known as the body and the soul. This topic will be studied at length in cosmology and metaphysics.

For the purposes of discussing logic, it is sufficient to examine certain types of perfections: faculties, habitus, and operations.

### Faculties

Substances do not merely be; they also do by performing a series of actions called operations. Even inanimate objects exert forces, emit or reflect radiation, and can be made to interact with other substances. As we go up the scale to nobler creatures, the number and perfection of operations increases. Substances that enjoy vegetative life have operations unknown to inanimate objects, such as growth, nutrition, and reproduction. Animals display sense knowledge and appetite, and most animals can move from place to place on their own power. Humans, called rational animals, can do everything that plants and animals can do, plus we are able to know things in themselves and to love them for their own sake.

In philosophy, plants are understood as corporeal substances capable of growth, nutrition, and reproduction but without sense or rational operations. These substances include many that are not in the plant kingdom as biology defines it, such as bacteria, archaea, fungi, and protists.

Save in the case of God, the raw essence of a substance is incapable of acting or doing anything on its own. In order for the substance to act in any way, it first needs a capacity to act in that way. That capacity is itself an accident or perfection of the substance. In order for an animal to move from place to place, it must first have a capacity for locomotion. Our capacity to know things in themselves is called the intellect and is absolutely necessary in order for us to make acts of knowledge. All of our operations have their root in some intrinsic capacity. Any capacity that produces an operation is called an active potency or active power. If the active potency produces an operation that only an animal can do, we call it a faculty.

In Thomistic and Aristotelian philosophy, God is perfectly identical with both His essence and His operations. God has no accidents, or more precisely, every perfection that for a creature would be an accident is God's very Essence.

When we distinguish the essence from the substance in metaphysics, by essence we mean that ontological principle that serves as the subject or substrate of the accidents. The essence is also the potential principle that receives the act of being.

This course on logic is most interested in the faculty that causes logic, which is the intellect: our capacity to know things as they are. It is possible to know in different ways. At its most fundamental level, to know something means that the knower, in a certain sense or part, actually becomes the thing known. The block of marble that Michelangelo formed into a statue came to know the model in Michelangelo's mind in a way. When a dog recognizes its owner, it does so by means of the sensible image formed in its internal senses. This image can be characterized as knowledge, specifically sense knowledge, which is knowledge acquired through internal and external senses without needing a properly rational faculty.

We now know through modern biology that the internal senses are located in the central nervous system, especially the brain.

In logic we are not directly interested in sense knowledge. Through sense knowledge, an animal can only gain access to the object insofar as it corresponds to or goes against its appetites. An animal knows something only insofar as it is perceived as beneficial or noxious. Human beings can do something greater: in addition to perceiving an object as beneficial or noxious, we can grasp what the object is in itself. A dog will wag its tail when its owner brings it food, but it is idle to ask the dog what food is or what an owner is, or whether these entities exist in reality. A human being has no trouble doing either. As a rational animal, a human being is capable of grasping two important aspects of the object of knowledge that are unavailable to an irrational animal: its essence (what that object is) and its being (whether or not that object exists). These aspects of rational knowledge form the basis for logic's object of study, which is the works of our reason. Through our concepts, we apprehend essences. Through our enunciations and the reasoning that we sometimes need to form enunciations, we make judgments regarding being.

C.S. Lewis in his book That Hideous Strength provides perhaps the best description of the difference between sense and rational knowledge. The passage describes how a bear's mind is as furry and unhuman in shape as his body, showing the absence of self-awareness and rational thought.

The distinction between sense and intellectual knowledge cannot be overemphasized. The distinction is not made by a large number of philosophers, especially those who derive their ideas from the Empiricist school. Great philosophers such as John Locke, David Hume, and Immanuel Kant can be constructively critiqued for overlooking this important point. Our capacity for intellectual knowledge and love, as distinct from sense knowledge and sense appetite, is the most important evidence in favor of the immateriality of our souls. Our intellects do not depend for their existence on our bodies but emanate directly from our rational, spiritual, immaterial souls. What confused the Empiricists is that our intellects, emanating from a soul that informs a body, require sensation as a necessary condition for their operation. It is true that there is nothing in the intellect that was not first in the senses. Nevertheless, intellectual knowledge is not produced by the body as sense knowledge is, but rather by a spiritual, immaterial faculty that transcends the body. The brain is not the organ that holds the intellect; rather, the intellect makes use of the brain and the rest of the body's sensory apparatus in order to function properly.

This is a good moment to make remarks about terminology. The most correct and proper name for the faculty through which we know things as they are is the intellect. The term mind is less precise because it can refer also to emotional states. Similarly, consciousness tends to imply that the person is actually exercising his intellect, whereas the intellect exists even when the person is asleep or otherwise unconscious. The intellect exists even before a person has developed the use of reason. Moreover, consciousness and self-awareness are difficult to define and could easily be attributed to some of the higher animals. The term reason is often used as a rough synonym for the intellect. When speaking in a broad sense, it is fine, but strictly speaking the term reason applies to our ability to take known premises and derive previously unknown conclusions from them. Only a human intellect can properly be called reason because the term refers to the fact that the human intellect is inherently discursive, meaning it uses internal words. The irrational animals do not have any intellect at all. The angels and God have a strictly intuitive intellect that does not require discourse.

There is an ambiguity in the way that Aquinas uses the Latin term intellectus. In most cases, he means the faculty by which we know things as they are, but in a few cases he is referring to an habitus by which we know the first principles of reason.

Logic studies neither the human intellect nor its operations, which would pertain to rational psychology or philosophical anthropology, but rather the works produced by that intellect. These works are of three kinds: concepts, enunciations, and arguments. Since we are attempting to study logic in a scientific way, and the intellect is the cause of those works, it is important to understand what the intellect is and what it does.

Faculties are the first accidents to emanate from a substance. They provide the substance with the basic capacities to do things and in general to act. They are the sine qua non for any operation whatsoever. Since we are studying logic, which studies the works of the human intellect, naturally, the faculty we will discuss the most is the intellect. We must now turn to the ways that faculties can be trained or deformed in order to function better or worse: that is, we must discuss habitus systematically.

### *Habitus*

We are now in a position to talk about habitus more systematically. In general, an habitus can be thought of as a stable ordering of a substance, which either perfects it or deforms it.

An analogy illustrates this concept: suppose a clean sheet of white paper has many possible configurations: it could be flat, rolled into a tube, folded, or crumpled into a ball. If I roll it into a tube gently without creasing it, I can easily go back to a flat sheet by uncurling it.

If instead I fold the paper neatly into three parts making two creases, I would have trouble going back to a flat sheet since the creases are hard to remove. On the other hand, the paper will never fit into a letter-sized envelope unless I first fold it.

This analogy helps us understand what in philosophy we call dispositions and habitus. When I roll up the paper gently, I change something about it, but the tube-shaped configuration is transitory. Similarly, dispositions are transitory perfections of a substance that can easily be undone. The creased fold causes a permanent change in the paper that is difficult to undo. Similarly, an habitus creates a state in the substance that is difficult to remove.

Some examples are in order. All human beings have the raw capacity to understand a science like quantum mechanics because they all have the intellect. However, most people do not immediately understand the Schrödinger equation, so our intellect needs training or formation to make accurate assertions about quantum physics. With some basic formation, we can acquire the correct opinion about certain facts, but this cannot be said to be a permanent and thorough assimilation. Armed with just that opinion, we will hardly be able to understand the two-slit experiment or the Pauli exclusion principle. To do that, one must be able to trace the phenomena to their causes. Only at that point is the assimilation secure and permanent, truly a scientia as we have understood the term. Right opinion about quantum physics is a disposition that enables the intellect to make some right assertions about the quantum world in a transient and often unreliable way. Acquiring the science of quantum mechanics gives secure, necessary, and permanent knowledge. Thus, a science is best described as an intellectual habitus that enables the one who has it to be secure in his knowledge of an object.

The intellect could also be malformed: one could gain false information about quantum physics, which could be transitory or deeply engrained. If the latter, it would consist in a kind of ignorance or prejudice: a deformation of the intellect. If this defect is durable and difficult to remove, it too is an intellectual habitus, but a bad one.

Four perfections (two of them better described as imperfections) of rational substances affect their ability to use their intellect: two dispositions (true opinion, false opinion) and two habitus (science and deep-seated prejudice) that train or malform the intellect. When an habitus affects operation like this, we call it an operative habitus. When that operative habitus is a good one, we call it a virtue. When it deforms that substance, we call it a vice.

There is no special name for good or bad dispositions.

Perhaps you are more familiar with applying the terms virtue and vice to morality. We often say that a person is virtuous if he behaves in a way that is in conformity with the natural law. It would be more precise to speak more specifically: a moral virtue is a good operative habitus that stably and durably helps a person to behave in a morally acceptable way, making such behavior easier and even pleasurable. In contrast, a moral vice is a bad operative habitus that stably and durably pushes the person to commit immoral acts. Each of these types of habitus also has a corresponding disposition, which helps the person to behave in a morally good or bad manner but in a transient and non-durable way.

A concrete example illustrates these concepts. Suppose one day at work I find a $20 bill on my desk that I strongly suspect belongs to my co-worker. I could really use that money, but after hemming and hawing, I decide that the morally good thing to do would be to return it. The decision was a close one; I nearly pocketed the money. I performed a morally good act, an act of honesty, which is a species of justice. However, I did so reluctantly. Suppose my colleague has a tendency to lose money, often leaving it carelessly on my desk. After a week, I have returned the money every time. After a month, it becomes practically second nature. Soon, I actually experience pleasure in doing so, especially given my colleague's gratitude. At the end of this process, I have acquired the moral virtue of honesty: I now have a stable and durable tendency to return lost money to its owner.

However, human acts are contingent: if I wanted to, I could act contrary to my conscience. Going back to the first day, suppose in this new timeline my greed gets the better of me and I decide to pocket the money. I don't get caught, and my colleague never discovers where he has left the money. At this point, I have committed a morally unacceptable act: an act of dishonesty (a type of theft). However, I have not yet acquired a stable tendency to act this way. Over time, I take advantage of my colleague's carelessness, pocketing hundreds of dollars over two months. I get better at figuring out where he leaves the money, even scheming to get him to drop more money, and I take pleasure in my newfound wealth. By now, I have acquired a stable disposition to theft, thus forming a vice.

In either case, with both the virtue and the vice, there is a period of transition during which it is still relatively easy to change course. In that intermediate stage, we can say that I have a disposition toward honesty or dishonesty, but not yet a virtue or a vice. Nevertheless, that disposition becomes stronger and more deeply seated as I continue to favor it with my action, until it hardens into an habitus.

These examples illustrate how operative habitus and dispositions work. They are principles of operation that have the function of perfecting a faculty, giving it the ability to do things that it was unable to do before, or else to do them with greater ease. They are halfway between the faculty and its operation: they partially actualize or determine the faculty, causing it to tend toward some operations over others.

Up to now, the habitus and dispositions discussed are all operative, meaning they are the principles of various kinds of operation. The focus has been on the operative kind because logic, our unique intellectual virtue that is simultaneously a speculative science, a practical science, and an art, is an intellectual operative habitus: evidently, it is a good one, hence an intellectual virtue. However, there is also a whole class of dispositions and habitus that are not immediately ordered to operation. Rather, they produce a state of being in the substance. These habitus and dispositions are called entitative. Health and physical beauty are both entitative dispositions. The sanctifying grace that Christians receive at Baptism is perhaps the best example of an entitative habitus.

Figure 2.1 shows the divisions of habitus and some examples of each.

Operative habitus all have elements in common, many of which they share with faculties. Both operative habitus and faculties are inherent in a subject or substrate. Both are principles or originators of operation. Both have an object, which is the finality or term to which the operation is directed.

From now on, for brevity's sake, I will speak only of habitus because that is the genus that logic falls under. However, all of these characteristics apply equally well to dispositions, unless otherwise specified. Dispositions are like habitus in all respects, except for durability.

Examples help illustrate these concepts. Looking at the faculty of sight, like all faculties, the subject or substrate will be the substance in which it is inherent (in this case, an animal, rational or irrational). We can specify that unlike purely immaterial faculties like the intellect, a sense like sight is dependent on the body or matter of the animal in question. The operation that sight produces is called vision. The object of sight can be described as colored material bodies insofar as they have color. Looking at the human intellect, being also a faculty, it will likewise have the substance as its subject (in this case, the human being in question). Unlike the sense of sight, it stems directly from the human being's immaterial soul, with no dependence on the body for its existence. Its operations include the simple apprehension of essences, and making judgments (mediated or immediate) regarding being. Its object can be described as the entities it comes to know, insofar as they are intelligible.

With operative habitus, it is important to specify what we mean by subject. Like all accidents, operative habitus inhere in the substance that they perfect, and so the substance could be regarded as their subject in that sense. However, more often, when we speak of the subject of an operative habitus, we mean the faculty that the habitus modifies. That is the meaning we will give the term in this introduction. Thus, all the intellectual habitus that we have talked about up to now, sciences and arts, have the intellect as their subject. If we look at quantum mechanics, its object is what the science studies: material entities as regards the behavior of the elemental particles that constitute them. The operations it produces consist in the acts of apprehending and judgment by the intellect regarding that object. Without the habitus of quantum physics, such apprehensions and judgments would be impossible; thus, the habitus is a true principle, or origin, of operation, albeit a secondary one that depends on the intellect and ultimately on the substance in which it inheres.

Similarly, if we look at an art, say carpentry, the subject is the intellect. The object is the works of carpentry produced, which consists of pieces of wood to be shaped into tables, chairs, and the like. The operations consist in the combination of knowhow and physical actions required to shape the wood into the products desired. Table 2.2 summarizes these examples with some additions.

### Operations

The effect of having faculties and habitus is the capacity for operation, which is simply the philosophical term for any actions that are produced by a substance. Operations always have their ultimate origin in the substance itself, but the faculty that produces them, together with any habitus that perfects it, can rightly be considered the immediate origins of that operation. In the oft-cited example, the scientist who contemplates Schrödinger's equation is rightly considered the author or origin of the act of contemplating. However, he engages in this operation by the mediation of his intellect, which has been perfected by the science of quantum physics.

Figure 2.2 illustrates the hierarchical relationship among substance, faculty, habitus, and operation. Operations can be produced entirely by the substance that acts, or else the substance may receive the power to act from an extrinsic source. An animal's capacity for locomotion is entirely intrinsic to itself. However, if it is trained to perform certain actions by a human master, it is acting based on training that it has received extrinsically. Similarly, a human being can do acts of knowledge and love under his own power. However, he can only do acts of supernatural knowledge (faith) and supernatural love (hope and charity) under the influence of grace, which he receives from God. Logic deals with the works of reason, which are the result of operations that are entirely intrinsic to man and do not require an extrinsic power. As shown in the diagram, all operations that are proper to the substance that produces them ultimately have their origin in the substance's actus essendi or act of being. This is a principle that you will learn about in your course on metaphysics. It is sufficient to say that it is a metaphysical principle that is the source of all proper actuality in a substance. The essence signifies the substance inasmuch as it is a metaphysical principle, a capacity that it fulfills in two ways: as the substrate for the accidents, and as the potency that receives the act of being. As such, the essence acts as a limit to and a measure of the intensity of the act of being. The first perfections to emanate from the substance are the active potencies, which include the faculties of sentient substances. Those faculties that entail a considerable freedom of action, especially the rational faculties in man, are further perfected by operative habitus.

All operations emanate from the substance's actus essendi. Those operations that are entirely intrinsic trace all of their actuality to this act of being; others require the assistance of an outside power. Regardless, operations are produced by the mediation of the substance's active powers. In sentient and rational beings, some of these powers, called faculties, are capable of a further perfection that we call operative habitus. Thus, a substance's operations pass through two and possibly three filters: the essence itself (which sets certain limits to what kinds of operations are possible), the faculties that produce the operations, and where this is possible, the habitus that perfect the faculties.

Every operation produces an effect, which we call its work or product. This work can be extrinsic to the substance that has produced it, for instance, the chairs and tables produced by the efforts of a carpenter. Or else it can be immanent, such as the internal effects of the actions of our intellect and will. It is important to note that the works or products of an operation are distinct from the operation itself. When it comes to logic, we will see that logic studies the works of the intellect, not the operations of the intellect, which would pertain to philosophical anthropology.

## The Operations and Works of the Intellect

Even though the object of logic is the works of reason, it is good for us to have an overview of what the intellect does. Fundamentally, the intellect, more specifically what Aquinas calls the possible intellect, is capable of accomplishing two kinds of operation: it can discover what something is, and it can discern whether something is the case. In more precise philosophical terms, the intellect can discover a thing's quiddity, and it can make a judgment regarding that quiddity's being: whether in fact that quiddity is or is not.

### Simple Apprehension

The species of intellectual operation that discerns what something is, we term the simple apprehension. To illustrate how this operation works, we can do a simple exercise. Suppose I attempt to make the following statement: ice cream.

This statement surely conveys some information to the reader; ice cream is an idea that we all understand. However, these words, by themselves, are insufficient to make an assessment regarding an actual state of affairs. You have no idea whether I am saying that I enjoy ice cream, or that chocolate ice cream is my favorite flavor, or that the ice cream that I have served you is melting. With the words ice cream, I convey merely a notion, an answer to the question what is it.

In our discussion above, we have already seen something that answers the question what is it: namely, the substance, or more precisely, the essence, which is the ontological principle in a substance that gives it its definition. Through a process that you will see in other courses, a faculty that Aquinas calls the agent intellect is able to abstract whatever is intelligible from the material entities that we encounter. This intelligible notion or definition is what actually answers the question what is it in our intellect, and thus we give it the technical name quiddity. It should be noted that the quiddity is not a distinct thing: it is not distinct from the object that is known. Rather, it is merely that object inasmuch as it is intelligible to us.

Ontologically speaking, only the substance can strictly be an essence; that is, only a subsistent entity can answer the question what is it. Material substances are the objects that our intellects most readily apprehend. However, we are also able to understand other entities, such as the accidents of substances, and through the use of negative judgments and analogy, even entities that transcend the material world, or that can only be understood by analogy. As a consequence, in logic (but not in metaphysics), it is necessary to expand our idea of quiddity to include notions that are not substances, and even some notions that are not completely unified: notions that are analogical rather than univocal.

The simple apprehension is what enables us to understand quiddities. The product or work of that operation we call the concept. The concept can also be called the universal (technically, the universale in praedicando), because a single concept allows us to know the quiddity of multiple individual things. It can be called an intention because it leads us toward the thing that is known. Ontologically, a concept is a passion of the soul: the immanent effect on the soul of the act of apprehending. Since the concept is one of the objects of our study, we will study it in much greater detail in class.

The Latin conceptus means that which has been conceived. The Latin intentio comes from roots meaning towards and to stretch out, to proceed, to strive.

### Judgement

The simple apprehension by itself leaves us with very incomplete knowledge: although it answers the question what, by itself, the apprehension tells us nothing about the actual state of affairs. Going back to our example, I can use the concept ice cream to make a series of propositions.

In each statement, I am uniting two distinct concepts, or as Aristotle would say, I am composing them. I am affirming that the concept on the right-hand side, which we call the predicate, truly is a reality that pertains to the concept on the left-hand side, which we call the subject. Subject and predicate are united, either explicitly or implicitly, by the verb to be, which we call the copula. By composing subject and predicate in this way, I am affirming the being of the predicate in the subject.

Observant readers will note that although each proposition has a single subject and a single predicate, some of the predicates are in fact compound: they unite two concepts into one. Our language makes shortcuts that greatly simplify the expression of our judgments.

However, suppose that instead, I make statements denying the being of the predicate. I have taken two concepts, which can be termed subject and predicate; however, this time, I have denied the being of the predicate. Logically speaking, instead of composing subject and predicate, I have divided or separated them.

From the discussion above, it is clear why Aristotle and Aquinas call this operation composition and division, because it always takes two concepts, a subject and a predicate, and either unites or separates them. It can also be termed the judgment, which was the terminology preferred in the Modern period. Like the simple apprehension, the judgment produces a work that is immanent to the soul, which we call an enunciation. Just as concepts are represented by terms, enunciations are represented verbally as propositions. It is only after making a judgment and producing an enunciation that it makes sense to talk about truth and falsehood. Those enunciations are true that correspond to reality, and likewise, those that are in disagreement with reality are false. If I affirm something that actually exists, then my affirmation is true; similarly, an enunciation denying something that does not exist is also true. However, if I affirm something nonexistent, or deny something that does exist, my enunciation is false. Once again, it is the product or work, immanent in the soul, that is the object of study of logic.

### Reasoning

When we make a judgment regarding being, we can arrive at it in two ways: either immediately, simply by seeing it, or by the mediation of judgments that we have previously made. Any judgment that I make regarding things that I can verify directly with my senses is immediate. The intellect, by acts of apprehension, generates the concepts and by means of a judgment constructs a relation of attribution between them, thus producing an enunciation. Producing that enunciation does not require anything except the apprehension of those concepts and verifying that they do in fact exist in reality. Suppose, however, that having learned about the biology of pine trees, I make an enunciation about chloroplasts. There is no direct way to verify the existence of chloroplasts, still less the fact that they tend to absorb red and blue light more than green light. Reaching this conclusion requires highly specialized instruments and a solid foundation in cell biology. It is impossible to make a judgment regarding the being of the chloroplasts without extensive use of previously established enunciations.

Attentive readers will note that some enunciations are actually compound enunciations that include a number of simpler ones joined together.

The discursive process that we use to make judgments that rely on previously obtained knowledge is called reasoning or argumentation. Argumentation entails constructing a series of arguments, which consist of two previously known enunciations called premises that are linked by a concept in common called the middle term. By linking the two enunciations and dropping the middle term, we produce a third enunciation called the conclusion. As with concepts and enunciations, arguments are internal, intelligible passions of the soul that are distinct from their external, verbal expressions, which we call syllogisms.

Reasoning made in real life will involve a great many arguments, but every reasoning can be broken down to simple arguments. In its verbal expression, such an argument is called a categorical syllogism. The intellect is not employing a third operation, exactly; it is still using the second operation, the judgment. What changes is merely the means that it uses to arrive at its judgment on being: that is, previous knowledge, as opposed to direct evidence.

According to some logic textbooks, there are three operations of the intellect: apprehension, judgment, and reasoning. In my opinion, this is not entirely correct. It is better to say that there are two operations: apprehension and judgment. However, in order to arrive at some judgments, arguments must be employed.

These works, concepts, enunciations, and arguments, form the basis of the three branches of logic: logic of the concept, logic of the enunciation, and logic of the argument or syllogism. Logic studies the works of reason, not its operations. What we will be discussing together are concepts, enunciations, and arguments; it is not our task in this course to consider apprehension and judgment, except insofar as these are the cause of the works. These works form the object, or as we will learn to call it, the material object of logic. We will discuss the object of logic in detail in the next chapter.
# The Definition of Logic

This chapter provides a detailed understanding of the definition of logic to answer the question "What is logic?"

Logic is defined as the instrumentally speculative and modally practical science whose material object is the works of reason, whose formal object quod is the relations of reason constructed between second-order intentions, and whose formal object quo is the light of the first principles of reason.

The necessary groundwork has already been laid in previous chapters. By the end of this chapter, readers should have a good understanding of what logic actually is.

## The Object

All faculties and operative habitus have an object, which refers to the entities that are referred to that faculty or habitus's operation.

The object of a faculty or habitus is that by reason of which all things are referred to that faculty or habitus. Vision's object is colored bodies insofar as they are colored.

The object practically defines the faculty.

The object of vision is colored material bodies (res colorata). These bodies constitute the set of all things that can be the object of sight. When a material entity becomes the object of our sight, it does so through a quality called color. Light is the means used to generate color and the medium that transmits it to eyes.

This analysis suggests three aspects of the object of sight: res colorata constitutes the material object of the sense of sight, color is the formal object quod, and light is the formal object quo.

In metaphysics, matter refers to a metaphysical principle that lacks definition or actualization by itself and requires an active principle (a form) in order to be something. Substances function as a sort of secondary matter in which the accidents are inherent. The human person is composed of prime matter (the body) that is informed by a substantial form called the soul.

The terms material object and formal object apply the metaphysical concepts of matter and form by analogy. The material object is "material" in the sense that by itself it lacks definition. Color is a formal object because it gives the material object its definition, and it is the formal object quod because color is that which our sight actually sees. Light is the formal object quo because it is the means by which we see, functioning as a kind of efficient cause of our seeing.

An art like carpentry is a kind of intellectual habitus that permits the artist to perform certain operations. The material object of carpentry is wood. The formal object quod of carpentry is wood insofar as it is capable of being shaped. The formal object quo consists of the various techniques that the carpenter uses to make finished products.

When the habitus in question is a science, the material object is the subject matter, or the set of things that the science studies. Biology studies living things, which constitute its material object. The formal object quod of a science is the particular aspect of the material object that is of interest to the science. Biology studies living things insofar as they are alive, so the formal object quod of biology is vegetative and animal life or bodily life. The formal object quo of a science is the method used; in the case of biology, this is the scientific method.

A science is often described using a formula such as "Science S studies entity E inasmuch as it has property X." This is a short way to define a science in terms of its material object and formal object quod.

There is one object of a given faculty or habitus. The three aspects discussed (material object, formal object quod, and formal object quo) are three aspects of a single object.

## The Material Object of Logic

The material object of logic is the works of reason: concepts, enunciations, and arguments. These are passiones animae, qualities of the soul that remain immanent there due to the operations of the intellect.

Logic studies the works of reason, not the operations of reason. We are interested in forming precise and well-defined concepts, properly formed and expressed enunciations, and correctly constructed arguments that are true in their conclusions. The manner in which the intellect functions does not enter the object as such; we are only interested in the intellect's operations as the causes of that object.

The more difficult issue is the formal object quod of logic: what aspect of the works of reason does logic focus on?

## The Formal Object Quod of Logic

The formal object quod of logic regards relations of reason that we construct between second-order intentions.

### Relations

A father and son are both substances metaphysically speaking. Once the father has begotten a son, he acquires a new perfection called fatherhood. The son simultaneously receives a perfection called filiation or sonship. These perfections are accidents, not substances. Aristotle considers these to be their own category of accident called pros ti or "toward which."

A relation is a reference that one substance has to another. This kind of relation is called a real relation to distinguish it from others.

The substance in which the relation is inherent is called the subject of the relation. The substance referred to is called the object. The relation is always based on some kind of foundation (quantity, action and passion, or some kind of knowledge). Real relations are normally bilateral or reciprocal. Relations are always conceived as bilateral by default by our intellect, even when the reciprocal relation does not really exist. In modern parlance, we refer to the pair of relations as a relationship. In metaphysical terms, what we call a relationship consists of two distinct relations, each one a perfection inherent in a distinct subject.

### Relations of Reason

A relation of reason (relatio rationis) is a construct that our reason treats as if it were a real relation, when in reality it is nothing at all.

In the proposition "John is himself," John is "divided" by our reason, and we consider two "instances" of him and attribute to each one a kind of relation. We are saying that John (in the subject) is the same as John (in the predicate), and vice versa. The relation of identity does not actually exist; it is a construct to help us understand that substances are united and permanent. Identity is a relation of reason, not a real relation.

Real relations based on knowledge illustrate relations of reason. When you read a book, the relation of having read the book is inherent in you, but the book is completely unchanged by you having read it. We conceive the relation of having been read alongside the real relation, but we use our judgment to deny that having been read actually exists; it is a relation of reason.

Relations of reason belong to a broader group of pure objects or constructs called entia rationis (entities of reason). An ens rationis refers to any construct that we treat as if it were an entity, when in reality it is not. It is a placeholder concept which, upon examination, does not refer to any actual thing in reality.

Hobbits are a fictional people imagined by Tolkien. There are no hobbits in reality; they are not essences or substances in the strict sense. Through our imagination, we can describe what such a people would be like if they did exist. Hobbits are figments with no actual ontological foundation. This analysis is valid for any fictional character or object.

Darkness is not really an entity; it is best characterized as the lack of light. Darkness does not exist as such, but dark rooms, dark nights, and dark clothing do. Similar things can be said of other privations: coldness, evil, blindness. In each case, we are hypostatizing the lack of a real thing.

A common thread among entia rationis with foundations in reality is that although each one is a pure object that does not exist, it has a precise foundation in reality. This suggests a twofold division of ens rationis: one according to how the ens rationis is attributed to a subject, and the other according to the way the ens rationis is constituted as a pure object of reason.

When "hobbit" is used as a predicate in "Frodo Baggins is a hobbit," the subject is completely nonexistent. When we say "The room is dark," the subject (room) is quite real, even though the attribute is not. We can divide entia rationis into those attributed to completely nonexistent subjects (ens rationis sine fundamento in re) and those whose subjects are real (ens rationis cum fundamento in re).

Among entia rationis cum fundamento in re, there are two ways to form them: either positively (based on an affirmation) or negatively (based on a negation). The negation of some real perfection is not truly a "something" but rather the lack of something. Aquinas argues that when we affirm something in an absolute sense, that affirmation must refer to something in reality. The only way to obtain an ens rationis through positive affirmation is to affirm it relatively, as a relation of reason.

The term ens rationis is ambiguous according to its verbal construction. It could refer to real entities that have reason as their efficient cause (the works of reason), in which case their dependence is effective. It could refer to real entities that have reason as their subject or substrate (intellectual habitus), in which case their dependence is subjective. It could refer to nonexistent entities that our reason apprehends as if they were entities, in which case their dependence is objective.

The relations of reason that interest us in logic are the relations that our reason constructs from one work of reason to another: from a concept to a concept, and from an enunciation to an enunciation. Examples include attribution or predication, being the subject to which a predicate is attributed, predicability, the relation of a species to the genus that contains it, and the relations of the major and minor premise to the conclusion. Since all of these relations have works of reason as their subject and object, they are relations of reason, not real relations. These relations will be the focus of study throughout the course.

### Intentions

The word intention in its most common modern usage references the movement of the will. Intention comes from the verb intendo, from the roots in (meaning "toward") and tendo (meaning "to stretch, proceed, or strive for"). Intendo refers to a movement toward something.

In Aquinas, intendo and the related term intentio can refer to movements of the will, but also to the operations of the intellect. It can refer to the concept insofar as it is directed to an object. Aquinas uses the term intentio sometimes as a synonym for the concept, not just as referring to the movement of the will.

It is possible to form a concept (an intention) of real entities, but it is also possible to form a concept of the works of our reason. When we make the statement "All human beings are mortal," the term human being refers to real flesh-and-blood human beings. We can also look reflexively on the works of reason that permitted us to make the statement.

Concepts that refer immediately to real entities are called first-order intentions. Concepts that refer to some work of our reason are called second-order intentions. Second-order intentions refer to reality in a mediate or indirect way.

The distinction between first-order and second-order intention is easier to understand with examples. In "All men are mortal," the concept man is a first-order intention because it refers to flesh-and-blood men. In "'Man' is a species of the genus 'animal,'" man is a second-order intention because it signifies the concept itself, not flesh-and-blood men.

A first-order intention is a concept that refers directly to a reality outside the soul. A second-order intention is an intention that refers to another intention or to some work of reason.

### Relations of Reason between Second-order Intentions

The formal object quod of logic is the relations of reason constructed between second-order intentions.

Logic in itself does not study realities that are outside the soul. Instead, it studies the works of reason as such, and as a consequence, we employ second-order intentions. Logic does not concern itself with the density of gold (that is for chemistry). Logic does concern itself with the fact that in the proposition "Gold has a density of 19.3 g/cm³," the concept gold is a species, it functions as the subject of the enunciation, and a predicate is attributed to it as a property. Logic deals specifically with the relations of reason that the intellect constructs from one second-order intention to another.

The logical relations we study are fundamentally of three kinds, which correspond to the three types of works of reason. Predicability describes concepts and concerns the ways in which a concept can be attributed to its subject; concepts can be univocal or analogical, and univocal concepts are predicable in one of five ways: as species, genus, difference, property, or accident. Predication concerns actual attribution of a predicate to a subject, constituting an enunciation; we divide enunciations according to their mode of attribution by quantity, quality, unity, and matter. Illation concerns the logical consequence of a conclusion from its premises, constituting an argument; illation can be deductive or inductive.

## The Formal Object Quo of Logic

The material object and formal object quod of logic have been discussed: what logic studies (the works of reason) and what specific aspect of the material object it is concerned with (the second-intention relations of reason). We must now discuss how logic is able to give us the ability to construct these relations correctly and bring us to the truth.

The first concept that the intellect makes is that of ens: as soon as the intellect is active, it realizes that there is something outside itself, and that this something is. We do not start out understanding ens in a formal or explicit sense. This knowledge is implicit, acquired by the very fact that our intellect is working (actu exercito).

As soon as we acquire our first concept (ens), our agent intellect causes us to form our first enunciation at the very same moment: namely that ens is not non-ens. Whatever is presenting itself to the intellect must be and cannot possibly not be. This judgment made actu exercito is called the principle of non-contradiction, the very first principle of reason.

The exercise of our intellect reveals a whole store of first principles: the principle of causality, the principle that the whole is greater than the part, the principle of identity, and so on. Aquinas argues that this set of principles constitutes a habitus called the habitus primorum principiorum. Since this habitus derives its efficacy from the very exercise of our reason, it is present in all human beings, infallibly, and without exception.

These principles make logic possible. It is defensible to call reason itself a kind of "natural logic" because of the first principles of reason.

## Putting All the Pieces Together

Logic is an instrumentally speculative and modally practical science. Logic fulfills the characteristics of both a science and an art. It is the means par excellence by which all other sciences attain true knowledge of the world outside the soul, but does not seek that kind of knowledge in itself, so it is instrumentally speculative. Logic helps other sciences obtain that knowledge by the correct construction of relations between the works of reason, so it is modally practical.

The material object of logic is the works of reason. Logic concerns itself with concepts, enunciations, and arguments: the fruit of the operations of our intellect. Logic studies the works of reason, not the operations of reason.

The formal object quod of logic is the relations of reason that can be constructed between second-order intentions. Logic looks reflexively on the works of reason and tells us how they are to be ordered if we are to acquire true and certain knowledge. It is all the other sciences that deal with first intentions.

What permits us to use our reason in a logical fashion (natural logic) and then to study the works of reason (scientific logic) is the first principles of natural reason. The formal object quo of logic is the light of the first principles.

Logic is the instrumentally speculative and modally practical science whose material object is the works of reason, whose formal object quod is the second-intention relations of reason constructed between those works, and whose formal object quo is the light of the first principles of reason.

## The Divisions of Logic

The works of reason (concepts, enunciations, and arguments) constitute the material object of logic. Classical logic is divided into three main chapters: the logic of the concept, the logic of the enunciation, and the logic of the argument or syllogism.

Getting to the truth is always the ultimate goal of logic. A large part of our work in logic involves understanding the formal conditions for obtaining the truth: if we presuppose that we are starting with sound principles and true knowledge, we can understand how to proceed to new knowledge starting from the old. This aspect of logic is called formal logic or minor logic: the part of logic that studies the correctness or validity of the relations of reason we construct.

Studying formal logic alone is incomplete. Consider a syllogism: "All animals have wings. All frogs are animals. Therefore, all frogs have wings." The syllogism is formally perfectly correct, but the conclusion is evidently false. The reason is that the major premise is false. Besides formal correctness, there is an additional condition to reaching a true conclusion: both premises must themselves be true. The conditions under which we can guarantee that formally correct reasoning leads to true conclusions are called the material conditions for truth, and their study is called material logic or major logic.

It is easy to apply the division between material and formal logic to the logic of the argument.

For the logic of the concept, the division applies in a way. Concepts are called universals because through one concept we can know many concrete individuals. There are two aspects of this universality: the manner in which the concept is attributed universally to its subject (predicability), and the kinds of objects or contents that the concept can refer to. The latter aspect constitutes the "material" conditions for the truth of the concept; the former constitutes the "formal" conditions. Thus, with the logic of the concept, the division between formal and material logic is somewhat justifiable, even though the criterion for dividing is rather different.

It is much more problematic to apply this division to the logic of the enunciation. When we attribute a predicate to a subject or separate it from that subject, we can do so either mediately (with the help of other enunciations that form our premises) or immediately (by understanding it directly from the first principles of reason or from experience). We obtain mediated knowledge through argument, and the logic of the argument is rightly divided between formal and material logic. What is left is immediate knowledge of a real object; by its very nature, immediate knowledge is not subject to questions about conditions for truth because it is already known to be true.
# Conclusion {-}

The book began by asking "What precisely is logic?" with precision and rigor. Readers have explored the place of logic among other arts and sciences. The book examined how logic perfects the intellect as an intellectual habitus. Readers should now be able to explain the definition of logic. Logic studies the works of reason as regards relations constructed between second-order intentions in light of the first principles of reason. Readers are now better prepared to explore the three great divisions of this science: the logic of the concept, the logic of the enunciation, and the logic of the argument.
